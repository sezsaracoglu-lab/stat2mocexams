Symptom → diagnosis → treatment

Every question type in this exam, in the order the papers meet them. Read the left column as “what the question says”, the middle as “what it actually is”, and the right as “what you do about it”.

📊 Proportions
A confidence interval and a claim that “there is no difference”
CI for a difference, read as a test
Is the null value inside? For a DIFFERENCE the null value is 0; for a RATIO or relative risk it is 1. Inside → not significant. Outside → significant, and the sign of the interval gives the direction.
“What is the margin of error?” or “give a 95% interval”
Confidence interval for one proportion
p̂ = X⁄n → SE = √(p̂(1−p̂)⁄n) → m = 1.96 × SE → interval = p̂ ± m. The margin is the ± part, not the whole width.
“What is the probability that a sample of n shows at least …?”
Sampling distribution of p̂
SE = √(p(1−p)⁄n) using the KNOWN population p → z = (target − p)⁄SE → read the table for the area to the LEFT → subtract from 1 if the question says “at least” or “more than”.
Two groups, two proportions, and the words “plus four”
Plus-four interval for p₁ − p₂
Add ONE success and ONE failure to EACH sample: p̃ = (X+1)⁄(n+2). Then (p̃₁ − p̃₂) ± 1.96 √(p̃₁q̃₁⁄ñ₁ + p̃₂q̃₂⁄ñ₂). For ONE proportion the correction is +2 and +2 instead.
🔗 Causality & design
A significant difference between two groups and a causal verb
Observational versus experimental
Ask who decided which group each person was in. If the participants did, you may say “is associated with” and nothing stronger. Name the specific threat: third variable, reverse causality, selection, or regression to the mean.
“What is this an example of?” after a colleague raises an objection
Naming the threat
New variable feeding both arms → third variable. Same two variables, arrow flipped → reverse causality. Groups defined by their own behaviour → selection. Extreme scores measured again → regression to the mean.
Two statements about covariation, temporal order or bidirectionality
The three criteria for causality
Covariation, temporal precedence, AND elimination of alternative explanations. Any statement offering only two of the three is false. Random assignment is what delivers the third.
🎲 Chi-square
One variable, k categories, a claim about how they should be spread
Chi-square goodness of fit
E = n⁄k (or n × claimed proportion) → χ² = Σ(O − E)²⁄E → df = k − 1 → compare with the table.
A grid of counts, two variables measured on the same people
Chi-square test of independence
E = row total × column total ⁄ grand total for each cell → χ² = Σ(O − E)²⁄E → df = (r − 1)(c − 1). Check the expected counts: the usual rule is all at least 5, with some texts allowing a few between 1 and 5.
A z-test and a chi-square test on the same 2 × 2 table
The χ² = z² identity
On a 2 × 2 table χ² = z² with df = 1 and the SAME p-value. Going back the other way, z = ±√χ², and the sign has to come from the observed proportions.
📈 Correlation & regression
An equation like ŷ = a + bx, or r with two standard deviations
Slope and intercept
If the equation is printed, the slope is the coefficient on x and the intercept is the lone number. If not, b = r × (s_y ⁄ s_x). To predict, substitute: ŷ = a + b × x.
A correlation and a claim about “how much is explained”
r versus r²
r gives direction and strength; r² gives the proportion of variance explained. Convert before comparing predictors, and remember r² loses the sign.
Individual-level and group-level correlations side by side
Aggregation and the ecological fallacy
Averaging within groups removes individual noise, so group-level r is almost always larger. A group-level correlation describes groups and must never be applied to a person.
A change of units, or a restricted sample
What moves r and what does not
Linear change of units: r unchanged, slope changes by the conversion factor. Negative multiplier: sign flips. Narrow the range of x: r falls. Spread x out (extreme groups): r rises. Non-linear transformation: r changes.
A described residual plot
Regression diagnostics
Fan or funnel → non-constant variance; the predictions survive, the p-values do not. Curve → non-linearity; the predictions are biased. Pattern in time order → non-independence. Q-Q plot or histogram → normality.
An ANOVA table with gaps, or SST, MSM and F given
Filling the table in either direction
SS ÷ df = MS for every row; F = MS_model ÷ MS_error; R² = SS_model ÷ SS_total. Backwards: SSM = MSM × k, SSE = SST − SSM, MSE = MSM ⁄ F, df_error = SSE ⁄ MSE, n = df_error + k + 1.
A question about intervals around a regression line
CI for the mean versus PI for an individual
The PI is always wider, because it carries an extra s² for individual scatter. Both pinch at x̄ and flare at the edges. As n grows the CI shrinks to nothing; the PI stabilises near ±2s.
SPSS output and “which statement is false?”
Reading output critically
R in the model summary has no sign — take it from the slope B. R² is the variance explained, never R. Read the Sig. column row by row. A small R² can still be highly significant.
🧪 One-way ANOVA
“All pairwise tests at α = .05” and a warning about alpha
Family-wise error rate
Comparisons = k(k − 1)⁄2. FWER ≈ 1 − 0.95^(comparisons), exactly so only if the tests were independent. Bonferroni tests each pair at α ÷ comparisons — divide by the number of COMPARISONS, not of groups.
MSE given, and the words “pooled standard deviation”
Recovering s from the table
MSE is the pooled VARIANCE within groups, so s_pooled = √MSE. If only SSE and df are given, divide first, then take the root.
“What are the degrees of freedom?”
df for every design
One-way: (k − 1, N − k), summing to N − 1. Two-way: (a−1), (b−1), (a−1)(b−1), N − ab, summing to N − 1. Repeated measures: df_residual = (k − 1)(participants − 1). Read them backwards too: F(a, b) means k = a + 1 and N = b + k.
A significant or non-significant omnibus F, and a conclusion to pick
Interpreting the F test
Significant → “at least one group differs”, never “all differ” and never “the biggest mean wins”. Not significant → “insufficient evidence”, never “no difference”, and check the power before saying anything more.
A non-significant result with a power figure quoted
Power and Type II error
Power = 1 − β. Low power plus a null result = uninformative. High power plus a null result = genuinely informative. More participants raise power without touching α; raising α does not count.
🔀 Factorial ANOVA
A two-way table of SS and df, one row asked for
F for a main effect or interaction
All three F values share ONE MS_error. Make the MS for the row you were asked about, then divide. A missing SS is found by subtraction from the total; the df row is your check.
A significant interaction, or a described plot
Interpreting factorial results
Significant interaction → describe it first and qualify the main effects; follow with simple effects. Plot: GAP between the lines = main effect of the line factor; SLOPE = main effect of the x-axis factor; NON-PARALLEL = interaction.
🔁 Repeated measures
A small table of people × conditions
Sums of squares in repeated measures
SST = Σ(x − grand mean)², whatever the design. Then: total → between-people + within-people; within-people → treatment + residual. F = MS_treatment ⁄ MS_residual, so the between-people part never reaches the denominator.
“Which test should be used?”
Test selection
Count the factors; count the levels; ask whether the same people appear at every level. One factor, 2 levels, same people → paired t. One factor, 3+ levels, same people → repeated-measures ANOVA. Different people → the independent versions. One factor of each kind → mixed ANOVA.
“X is the repeated-measures variant of …” or “t² = …”
Relationships between the tests
t² = F whenever the numerator has one df. χ² = z² on a 2 × 2 table. Paired t = one-sample t on the difference scores. Levene’s test is about VARIANCES and belongs to none of these pairs.
Order, Latin squares, or “why was this done?”
Counterbalancing
Within-subjects designs are more sensitive but create order effects. Counterbalancing spreads each condition evenly across positions so practice, fatigue and novelty cancel. k conditions → k! orders. It cannot fix an asymmetric carry-over.
⚖️ Ethics & inference
Fabrication, falsification, p-hacking, HARKing or selective reporting
Research ethics
Invented data → fabrication. Altered or hidden data → falsification. Trying analyses until one works → p-hacking. Writing an exploratory finding up as predicted → HARKing. All of them inflate the real error rate and corrupt the shared record.
Two labelled statements about inference
Types of inference
“Beyond chance” → statistical, supported by the test. “Caused / improved / influenced” → causal, supported only by random assignment. “Will also work elsewhere” → external validity, supported by who was sampled.

A confidence interval turned into a verdict

Educational researchers compare two schools on the percentage of pupils who read for pleasure. The 95% confidence interval for the difference in proportions (school B − school A) runs from 0.04 to 0.22. The head teacher of school A, which has more difficulties, claims there is no real difference between the schools. What is your conclusion?
  • aThe head teacher is incorrect — there is a significant difference
  • bThe head teacher is correct — the interval is too narrow to show anything
  • cYou cannot tell without the p-value
  • dThe head teacher is correct — the interval contains plausible values near zero
🔎 What it is really asking

It looks like a question about school politics. It is not. It is asking one mechanical thing: does this interval contain zero?

🧭 Which type of question this is

A confidence interval for a DIFFERENCE, read as a two-sided test at α = .05.

🛠️ The procedure

For a difference between two groups, the null value is 0 ("no difference"). If the 95% CI excludes 0, then 0 is not a plausible value for the true difference, and you reject H₀ at the 5% level. If the CI contains 0, you do not reject.

🧮 The working
The interval runs 0.04 → 0.22. Both ends are positive, so every plausible value of the true difference is a real difference, and the smallest one still says school B is 4 percentage points ahead. Zero is nowhere in the interval. So H₀: p_B − p_A = 0 is rejected, and the head teacher is wrong.
✗ Why the others are wrong
b.Width has nothing to do with it. A narrow interval that excludes zero is the STRONGEST kind of evidence, not the weakest.
c.You do not need one. A 95% CI that excludes the null value is a two-sided test at α = .05 — exactly so for means, and closely enough for proportions.
d.The interval does not contain zero itself, and that is the only criterion. Its smallest plausible value is still a four-point advantage to school B.
🏷️ Difference → null value is 0. Ratio or relative risk → null value is 1.

Question 2

🔗 Causality & design

Correlation is not causation

Psychologist Tom investigates whether gaming for four or more hours a day is linked to sleep problems in 450 young people. He finds that 60% of heavy gamers report sleep problems compared with 34% of non-gamers, p = .003. Tom concludes that excessive gaming causes sleep problems. What is wrong with this conclusion?
  • aHe should have used a chi-square test instead of comparing proportions
  • bThis is correlational research, so it cannot establish causation
  • cThe p-value is not small enough to support a causal claim
  • dThe sample is too small for a conclusion about young people
🔎 What it is really asking

You are being asked to spot the single most common error in the whole course: reading a difference between two self-selected groups as a cause.

🧭 Which type of question this is

Design critique — observational versus experimental.

🛠️ The procedure

Ask one question: were people ASSIGNED to the groups by the researcher, or did they arrive in them on their own? Tom did not hand anyone a console. Young people chose how much to game, so the two groups differ in every other way as well.

🧮 The working
A significant p tells you the 26-point gap is unlikely to be chance. It says nothing about WHY the gap exists. Three explanations survive: gaming disturbs sleep; poor sleepers stay up gaming (reverse causality); or something else — anxiety, screen habits, household chaos — produces both (a third variable). Only random assignment rules out the last two.
✗ Why the others are wrong
a.Comparing two proportions is perfectly appropriate here, and a chi-square on this 2 × 2 table would give the same p. The problem is the conclusion, not the test.
c.p = .003 is very small indeed. No p-value, however small, converts a correlation into a cause — that is the whole point.
d.n = 450 is comfortable. Sample size affects precision, never the type of inference you are entitled to.
🏷️ No random assignment → no causal verb. Say “is associated with”, never “causes”.

📊 Proportions

Margin of error for one proportion

📝 the numbers were chosen to fit the key
School psychologist Petra screens 87 primary-school pupils for dyslexia and 44 of them screen positive. She wants a 95% confidence interval. What is the margin of error?
  • a0.210
  • b0.0029
  • c0.105
  • d0.054
🔎 What it is really asking

"Margin of error" is the ± part of the interval. It is not the interval, and it is not the standard error.

🧭 Which type of question this is

Margin of error for a single proportion.

🛠️ The procedure

m = z* × SE, where SE = √( p̂(1 − p̂) / n ) and z* = 1.96 for 95%.

🧮 The working
p̂ = 44 ⁄ 87 = 0.506.
SE = √( 0.506 × 0.494 ⁄ 87 ) = √0.002873 = 0.0536.
m = 1.96 × 0.0536 = 0.105.
The interval itself would be 0.506 ± 0.105 = (0.401, 0.611) — but the question only asked for the ±.
✗ Why the others are wrong
a.0.210 is the full WIDTH of the interval (two margins). The margin is half of it.
b.0.0029 is the variance p̂(1−p̂)⁄n, left un-rooted.
d.0.054 is the standard error, before multiplying by z*. A very common slip.
📝 THE NUMBERS WERE CHOSEN TO FIT THE KEY

The keyed answer is 0.105, and the sample was chosen to produce it exactly: 44 of 87 pupils gives SE = 0.0536 and a margin of 0.105. With different figures, the procedure is what carries over.

🏷️ SE → ×1.96 → margin → ± around the estimate. Four steps, always in that order.

Probability from the sampling distribution of p̂

📝 the numbers were chosen to fit the key
Nationally, 8% of students have a diagnosed learning disability. Study adviser Fatima welcomes a group of 200 new first-year students. What is the probability that at least 10% of her group has a learning disability?
  • a0.8508
  • b0.4706
  • c0.3300
  • d0.1492
🔎 What it is really asking

Not "is this significant?" but "how often does a sample of 200 land this high by chance?" You are being asked for an area in the tail of the sampling distribution.

🧭 Which type of question this is

Normal approximation to the sampling distribution of a sample proportion.

🛠️ The procedure

The sampling distribution of p̂ is centred on p with SD = √( p(1−p)/n ). Standardise the value you care about, then read the table. Note: this uses the KNOWN population p = .08, not a sample estimate.

🧮 The working
SE = √( 0.08 × 0.92 ⁄ 200 ) = √0.000368 = 0.0192.
z = (0.10 − 0.08) ⁄ 0.0192 = 1.04.
The table gives the area to the LEFT of 1.04 as .8508.
“At least 10%” is the right tail: 1 − .8508 = 0.1492.
✗ Why the others are wrong
a.0.8508 is the area to the LEFT of z = 1.04 — the probability of FEWER than 10%. Read the table, then ask which side you wanted.
b.0.4706 is what you get by dividing by √(p(1−p)) = 0.271 and forgetting the ⁄n altogether: z = 0.07, a tail of almost one half.
c.0.3300 is the tail beyond z = 0.44, which comes from a slip in computing the standard error.
📝 THE NUMBERS WERE CHOSEN TO FIT THE KEY

The comparison rate used here is 8%: with the 200 students and the 10% threshold it gives z = 1.04 and a tail of exactly .1492.

📊 Proportions

Plus-four confidence interval for two proportions

⚠ the key stands, but check the note
Are parents of girls more inclined to pay for extra tuition? A simple random sample of 50 girls and 45 boys was taken at a large university on the west coast. 20 girls and 21 boys report receiving it. What is the 95% plus-four confidence interval for the difference in population proportions, with girls as group 1?
  • a(0.176, 0.240)
  • b(−0.260, 0.131)
  • c(−0.064, 0.131)
  • d(−0.195, 0.195)
🔎 What it is really asking

A two-proportion interval with the plus-four correction — which for TWO samples is different from the one-sample version.

🧭 Which type of question this is

Plus-four confidence interval for p₁ − p₂.

🛠️ The procedure

Add ONE success and ONE failure to EACH sample, so p̃ = (X + 1) ⁄ (n + 2) for each group with ñ = n + 2. Then (p̃₁ − p̃₂) ± z* √( p̃₁(1−p̃₁)/ñ₁ + p̃₂(1−p̃₂)/ñ₂ ).

🧮 The working
Keyed answer: (0.176, 0.240).

Running the procedure on the counts as printed:
p̃₁ = 21 ⁄ 52 = 0.4038   p̃₂ = 22 ⁄ 47 = 0.4681
difference = −0.0642
SE = √( 0.4038 × 0.5962 ⁄ 52 + 0.4681 × 0.5319 ⁄ 47 ) = 0.0996
margin = 1.96 × 0.0996 = 0.1953
which gives (−0.260, 0.131) — an interval containing zero.

So the printed counts and the keyed answer do not match. See the note below; the procedure above is the thing to carry into the exam either way.
✗ Why the others are wrong
b.(−0.260, 0.131) is what the plus-four formula produces from 20⁄50 and 21⁄45. It is keyed here as a distractor because the key marks (0.176, 0.240) — read the note.
c.(−0.064, 0.131) runs from the point estimate to the upper bound. An interval has to extend the same distance either side of the estimate.
d.(−0.195, 0.195) is centred on zero rather than on the observed difference.
⚠ THE KEY STANDS, BUT CHECK THE NOTE

The keyed answer is (0.176, 0.240). But it cannot be the plus-four interval for 50 and 45 people, and the reason is arithmetic rather than opinion: that interval is 0.064 wide, so the margin is 0.032 and the standard error 0.0163. With these sample sizes the NARROWEST margin obtainable from any counts at all is 0.056 — and a margin of 0.032 would need roughly 1700 people per group.
So the keyed interval must come from different sample sizes. Here, pick (0.176, 0.240). With any other numbers, run the procedure.

🏷️ One proportion: +2 successes and +2 failures. TWO proportions: +1 success and +1 failure to EACH sample. Different corrections — the exam tests exactly this.

Question 6

🔗 Causality & design

Naming the threat to a causal claim

A study adviser investigates whether attending class relates to passing it. Among attenders 35% more pass, p = .018. A colleague suggests that students who are more motivated both attend lectures more often and pass more often. What is the colleague’s suggestion an example of?
  • aA Type II error — a real association that the study has missed
  • bA third variable — an alternative explanation for the association
  • cReverse causality — passing the course is what drives attendance
  • dA Type I error — the association itself is a chance finding
🔎 What it is really asking

You must name the specific threat. The exam distinguishes four of them and they are easy to blur.

🧭 Which type of question this is

Threats to causal inference — naming, not computing.

🛠️ The procedure

Third variable: something OUTSIDE the pair causes both. Reverse causality: Y causes X instead of X causing Y. Type I: you called a real nothing something. Type II: you missed a real something.

A small pilot almost always has low power, which means β is high and a real effect is likely to be missed. The trouble is not the miss itself but the interpretation: a null result from an underpowered study is uninformative, yet it is routinely written up as “no effect”.
The other three are all desirable features.
✗ Why the others are wrong
a.A low alpha controls false positives, which is a virtue — though it does cost power.
b.A large effect size makes detection easier, which helps rather than hinders.
d.High power is exactly what you want.
🏷️ Small pilot + null result = no conclusion. Report the achieved power alongside it.

Question 35

🔁 Repeated measures

Partitioning sums of squares in repeated measures

In a repeated-measures ANOVA with 10 participants and 4 conditions, what are df_model and df_residual?
  • a3 and 36
  • b9 and 27
  • c4 and 30
  • d3 and 27
🧮 The working
df_total = N − 1 = 40 − 1 = 39.
df_between-people = 10 − 1 = 9.
df_within-people = 39 − 9 = 30.
df_model (conditions) = k − 1 = 3.
df_residual = 30 − 3 = 27, which also equals (k − 1)(participants − 1) = 3 × 9 = 27.
So you would report F(3, 27).
✗ Why the others are wrong
a.36 would be the error df for an INDEPENDENT ANOVA on 40 observations (40 − 4) — which is the df the repeated-measures design saves you from using.
b.9 is df_between-people, not df_model.
c.4 is k, not k − 1, and 30 is df_within-people before the treatment is removed.
🏷️ df_residual = (conditions − 1) × (participants − 1). Compare it with N − k to see what the design costs and buys.

Question 36

🔗 Causality & design

Within-subjects versus between-subjects designs

A within-subjects study finds a significant effect. A reviewer worries about order effects. What is the strongest reply?
  • aThe order was counterbalanced, so position effects fall equally on all conditions
  • bOrder effects do not occur in within-subjects designs
  • cThe sample size was large, so order effects average out automatically
  • dA significant result shows that order effects cannot have been present
🧮 The working
Counterbalancing is the design feature that answers this objection directly: by spreading each condition evenly across every position, practice, fatigue and novelty affect all conditions equally and cannot masquerade as a treatment effect.
If counterbalancing was NOT used, the reviewer’s concern stands and there is no statistical repair.
✗ Why the others are wrong
b.They are the characteristic weakness of within-subjects designs, not absent from them.
c.A large sample does not help if every participant meets the conditions in the same order — the bias is systematic, not random.
d.A significant result is perfectly compatible with an order effect having caused it.
🏷️ The answer to “what about order effects?” is always “we counterbalanced”.

Question 37

🔗 Causality & design

The three criteria for causality

In which of these can temporal order be established most convincingly?
  • aA retrospective study where participants recall past exposure
  • bAn experiment where the manipulation happens before the outcome is measured
  • cA cross-sectional survey measuring both variables at a single time point
  • dA correlational study where one variable is logically prior to the other
🧮 The working
Temporal order means the cause demonstrably came first. In an experiment the researcher creates the cause at a known moment and measures the outcome afterwards, so the ordering is a matter of record rather than inference.
The other designs rely on argument, on logic, or on memory — all weaker.
✗ Why the others are wrong
a.Retrospective recall is vulnerable to memory bias, particularly when the outcome is already known.
c.Measuring both at once gives no information about which came first. This is the weakest design on this criterion.
d.A logical argument about priority is not evidence of it.
🏷️ Experiments settle temporal order by construction. Everything else argues for it.

Question 38

⚖️ Ethics & inference

Statistical inference versus causal inference

A randomised trial in one hospital finds the treatment works (p = .002). Which claim goes beyond what the study can support?
  • aThe difference observed is unlikely to be the product of chance
  • bThe size of the benefit can be estimated with a confidence interval
  • cThe treatment will work equally well in community clinics with different patients
  • dThe treatment caused the improvement in this trial
🧮 The working
Randomisation secures the CAUSAL inference within this trial, and the p-value secures the STATISTICAL one. Neither secures the claim that the result transfers to a different setting and a different kind of patient — that is EXTERNAL validity, and it depends on who was studied, not on how well the study was run.
A perfectly executed trial in one hospital can still fail to generalise.
✗ Why the others are wrong
a.Supported — that is what p = .002 says.
b.Supported — intervals are available for any estimate.
d.Supported — random assignment is what licenses the causal verb.
🏷️ Internal validity (did it work here?) and external validity (will it work there?) are separate questions.

Question 39

⚖️ Ethics & inference

Research ethics

Why does the replication crisis in psychology connect to questionable research practices?
  • aReplication failures are caused mainly by the outright fabrication of data
  • bReplication failures show that statistical significance is a meaningless idea
  • cReplications are usually run by less skilled researchers than the originals
  • dp-hacking and HARKing produce false positives, which then fail to replicate
🧮 The working
p-hacking (trying analyses until one works) and HARKing (writing up an exploratory result as if it had been predicted) both inflate the true Type I error rate far above the stated 5%. The literature then fills with findings that were chance all along — and chance does not repeat.
The remedies are structural: pre-registration, reporting all outcomes, and adequately powered designs.
✗ Why the others are wrong
a.Outright fabrication is rare. The bulk of the problem is ordinary analytic flexibility, which is far more common and far less obviously wrong.
b.Significance is meaningful when the procedure is followed honestly. The problem is the procedure, not the concept.
c.There is no evidence for this, and large coordinated replication projects have been conducted with great care.
🏷️ Most false positives come from flexible analysis, not from fraud.

Question 40

🔁 Repeated measures

The purpose of counterbalancing

With three conditions, how many possible orders exist, and what does complete counterbalancing require?
  • a6 orders, and equal numbers of participants assigned to each of them
  • b3 orders, and equal numbers of participants assigned to each of them
  • c9 orders, and one participant assigned to each of them
  • d3 orders, since only the first position needs to be balanced
🧮 The working
The number of orders of three conditions is 3! = 3 × 2 × 1 = 6: ABC, ACB, BAC, BCA, CAB, CBA.
Complete counterbalancing uses all six with equal numbers of participants in each, which needs n to be a multiple of 6.
When that is impractical — four conditions give 24 orders, five give 120 — a Latin square is used instead, balancing each condition across positions without running every permutation.
✗ Why the others are wrong
b.3 is the number of conditions, not the number of orders.
c.9 is 3², which counts something else entirely.
d.Balancing only the first position leaves the second and third confounded.
🏷️ k conditions → k! orders. Beyond four conditions, use a Latin square.
